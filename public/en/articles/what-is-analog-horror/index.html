What Is Analog Horror? Meaning, Features, Key Works and Viewing Cautions

Published: 2026-10-03

A screen of static, a faded weather report, a sudden emergency broadcast. Analog horror uses the grainy texture of old television and videotape to build dread slowly. Born from independent YouTube projects, it reached cinemas worldwide in 2026 with the film The Backrooms. Here are its features, key works, and what to keep in mind when watching.

What Analog Horror Is

Analog horror depicts fear through the rough, eerie texture of old television broadcasts and video footage. Most works are short series made by individuals or small teams and released free on YouTube. They digitally recreate the flaws of analog video, such as grain, noise, static and shaking, and present them in the 4:3 frame of old TVs.

Common formats include TV station broadcasts, training videos, documentaries and children's programs. Synthetic narration and familiar-looking stock footage are often used, and looking like a genuine old record is the foundation of the fear.

Common Techniques

Many analog horror works begin with ordinary footage and grow more unsettling bit by bit. There are few characters, and the viewer on this side of the screen often feels like the target; broadcasts taken over in the form of emergency alerts are a recurring device. Background music is rarely used, and endings are often left ambiguous.

According to YouTube's official blog, analog horror videos were viewed more than 500 million times between January and August 2024 alone. The same blog lists "wandering liminal spaces" as one of the genre's common techniques.

Key Works

Kris Straub's Local 58 (2015–) is credited with establishing the genre's conventions. It presents the broadcasts of a fictional local TV station being hijacked by something over decades, and the name "analog horror" is said to have spread from its tagline.

Gemini Home Entertainment (2019–) by Canada's Remy Abode takes the form of an anthology of old videotapes. Alex Kister's The Mandela Catalogue (2021–) is set in a fictional county and features beings that imitate human appearance. Some works, like The Walten Files, begun in 2020, draw on video games.

Then in 2022, Kane Parsons (Kane Pixels), then 16, released a series about the Backrooms. Linking analog horror with liminal spaces, it led to the later film.

Where It Came From

Frightening audiences by mimicking real footage has precedents. Ghostwatch, a 1992 BBC television film presented as a live paranormal broadcast, is cited as a forerunner of analog horror. Found-footage films, which present handheld camera material as a record, are a related lineage.

The influence of internet ghost stories, or creepypasta, is also noted. Local 58's creator, Straub, wrote "Candle Cove" in 2009, a story about a strange children's program people half-remember, which was later adapted for American television.

From YouTube to Cinemas

A24's The Backrooms, directed by Kane Parsons, opened in the US in May 2026 and became A24's highest-grossing film. It opened in Japan on September 4, 2026, where the publicity foregrounded the term "liminal space."

In July 2026, a film adaptation of The Mandela Catalogue was also confirmed, produced by Steven Spielberg's Amblin and directed by Kister himself. It has been reported for release in summer 2028.

In Japan: Fake Documentaries as a Related Trend

The term is beginning to be known in Japan, introduced for example by the film news site Eiga.com. Similar Japanese works, however, are more often called "fake documentaries" or "mockumentaries," such as the YouTube series Fake Documentary Q and TV Tokyo's TXQ FICTION.

These share with analog horror the approach of stirring unease by looking like genuine programs or records.

Its Relationship to Liminal Spaces

Analog horror and liminal spaces are overlapping neighbors. If liminal spaces are the unease of empty real places, analog horror is the unease of old media records. Both create anxiety and nostalgia from something familiar that is slightly off.

Many analog horror works linger on empty spaces, and Kane Parsons' Backrooms is where the two overlap most deeply.

Cautions for Viewers

Some analog horror is intense. The Mandela Catalogue, for example, includes depictions suggestive of suicide and of bodily harm. If you are sensitive to such content or feeling low, there is no need to watch.

Many works that look like children's programs are made for adults. In 2022, YouTube mistakenly changed the age setting of a Local 58 episode titled as a show for children to "made for kids," and the creator had to appeal to fix it.

Touching Analog Time in Real Places

Something close to on-screen analog eeriness survives in old real places. Higashi-Narita Station in Chiba opened in 1978 as "Narita Airport Station"; on its disused platform, signs with the old name and old advertisements remain.

Rosa Kaikan in Ikebukuro is an amusement building opened in 1968, whose winding corridors and old signs still carry the air of Showa-era entertainment. LIMap records places like these with photos and locations.

FAQ

What is analog horror?

A YouTube-born horror genre that uses the grainy texture of old TV broadcasts and videotapes. Formats such as hijacked broadcasts, training videos and children's programs are common.

What are the key works?

Kris Straub's Local 58 (2015–), Remy Abode's Gemini Home Entertainment (2019–), Alex Kister's The Mandela Catalogue (2021–), and Kane Parsons' Backrooms series (2022–), among others.

Is it suitable for children?

It depends on the work, but many are intense, and some that look like children's shows are made for adults. Works like The Mandela Catalogue include depictions suggestive of suicide, so take care.
