Abandoned Schools as Liminal Spaces: 9 Real Empty Schoolhouses in Japan

Published: 2026-10-04

The schools everyone attended, with only the children's voices gone. An empty school overlaps with the one in your memory, bringing nostalgia and unease at once. Here is one you can tour inside and eight you can view from outside, with the rules to keep.

Why Empty Schools Feel Liminal

A school is a place almost everyone attended for years. Identical classrooms line the corridors, and even the arrangement of blackboard, desks and windows is much the same everywhere. That is why a school building emptied of children's voices overlaps with the school in your own memory, bringing nostalgia and unease at the same time.

In Japan, small branch schools and town schools have closed each time the number of children fell or people left mining and pioneer settlements. Where the buildings still stand, only the sense that children were truly there remains.

This article introduces abandoned schools registered on LIMap: one you can tour inside, and eight whose exteriors you can see from roads or public places. Most are on private or restricted land, and the buildings are in poor condition. It does not describe how to get inside, and you should not go in.

A Wooden School You Can Enter: Shakunage Gakko (Hino, Shiga)

The former Kaigake Elementary School in Hino, Shiga, is a wooden building from 1930. After it closed in 2001, a local nonprofit has preserved it as "Shakunage Gakko" and opened it to the public. You can actually walk through classrooms that still hold faded blackboards, old desks and chairs, and textbooks from the time.

The corridor, where only the creak of the wooden floor echoes, is an empty after-school building itself. It has also served as a location for many dramas, including NHK morning serials. It is open on Saturdays, Sundays and holidays from 10:00 to 16:00 and closed on weekdays, with a maintenance contribution of 300 yen. It is hard to reach by public transport, so check the opening days before you go.

Schools of the Mining Towns: Shoto Elementary's Round Building, Asahi Elementary (Hokkaido)

The Garo district of Bibai thrived with the Mitsubishi Bibai coal mine. At its peak more than 1,800 children attended Shoto Elementary School, and in 1959–60 two three-story round buildings of reinforced concrete were built. Joined by a connecting corridor, the two circles are said to have looked like a pair of glasses from above. The school closed in 1974 after the mine shut, and one round building and the gym remain in the forest. Water pools on the ground floor and freezes in winter.

The Shoto site, however, is deep in the forest, not by a road, and is not a public facility. The area is brown bear habitat, and there are "beware of bears" signs on site. The building's floors have given way and it is close to collapse, so it is safest not to approach and to know it only through photos and the map.

The former Asahi Elementary School in Yubari stands right across from the Yubari Coal Mine Museum. It opened in 1975 when three elementary schools merged, using the old Yubari No. 2 Elementary building, but only eight years later, in 1983, it was merged into another school and closed. It was then used as the lodging "Family School Fureai" until 2006. The building with broken windows still carries the facility's sign, and the gym roof collapsed in heavy snow in 2021. When you visit the museum, you can see its exterior from the surrounding streets.

Northern Schools Left in the Wilds: Haboro Midori no Mura (Taiyo Elementary), Nakahoronuka Branch School (Hokkaido)

Taiyo Elementary School, on the site of the Haboro coal mine in the mountains of Haboro, opened in 1940. In 1962 an unusual round gym 32.7 meters across was built. After the mine closed, the school shut in 1971, was used from 1982 as the town's lodging "Midori no Mura," and closed again in 2001. The famous round gym was crushed by the weight of snow in March 2018, leaving the three-story concrete school building and the wreckage of the gym.

The Nakahoronuka Branch School in Rumoi began as Nakahoronuka Elementary School, opened in 1905. As children dwindled it became a branch school in 1971 and closed in 1983, with one teacher and one pupil at the end. The wooden schoolhouse and teacher's residence still stand by the prefectural road, but the classroom ceilings have fallen in and the walls are collapsing.

Brown bears roam both areas. The bridge to Taiyo Elementary is chained off to vehicles. Do not approach the buildings; view them only from the road.

Small Wooden Branch Schools in the Hills: Soro, Aioi and Nagura (Chiba, Gifu, Aichi)

The Soro Jinjo Elementary branch school in Kamogawa, Chiba, is a wooden schoolhouse that opened in 1874, became a branch school through a merger in 1909, and closed in 1967. Children from districts far from the main school came here, among them Mizuta Mikio, who later served as finance minister and founded Josai University; a monument stands on the grounds. The floors and roof have collapsed and the grounds are off-limits, so view it from Prefectural Route 89 or the neighboring shrine.

The Aioi branch of Aioi Elementary School in Hachiman, Gujo, Gifu, opened in 1909, and its two-story wooden building from 1954 still stands. It closed in 1970 and was later used as a community hall. It is known for stairs on which each step was labeled with a place value such as "millions" and "ten millions." It is now privately managed and off-limits, and a house stands right next door. View it quietly from the prefectural road.

In Shitara, Aichi, there are two former branches of Nagura Elementary School. The Shimizu branch is a single-story wooden schoolhouse that opened in 1948 and closed in 1964, standing within a hamlet. The Komagahara branch served a pioneer settlement that people returning from Manchuria carved out of a plateau about 1,000 meters up after the war; its 1953 building was closed temporarily in 1978 and abolished in 1982. A campsite is next door. At both, view from the road so as not to disturb people nearby.

Rules to Keep When Visiting Abandoned Schools

Most abandoned schools are land and buildings owned by municipalities, individuals or companies. An open gate or broken window does not mean you may enter. Floors and roofs of wooden schools give way easily, and concrete buildings are damaged by snow and water.

Branch schools in the hills are almost always in or next to hamlets where people still live. Do not park on the road or point your camera at houses or people. Bears appear in Hokkaido and mountain areas, and snow can close roads in winter. If you want to see inside, choose a place that is open to the public, like Shakunage Gakko.

If You Find One, Put It on the Map

Abandoned schools crumble a little each year under snow and rain, and one day they are gone. Some have already changed, like Taiyo Elementary, whose round gym collapsed. The regional articles and "Abandoned Hotels as Liminal Spaces" introduce other places too.

FAQ

Are there abandoned schools I can go inside?

Shakunage Gakko (the former Kaigake Elementary School) in Hino, Shiga, is open on Saturdays, Sundays and holidays from 10:00 to 16:00, and for a 300-yen contribution you can tour the wooden building. Most other abandoned schools are private or off-limits, so view them only from outside.

Can I visit the round building of Shoto Elementary School?

It is deep in the forest, not by a road, and is not a public facility. It is brown bear habitat and the building is close to collapse, so do not try to approach it.

What should I keep in mind when photographing abandoned schools?

Do not enter the grounds; shoot from roads and other places open to everyone. Branch schools in the hills are often in hamlets, so keep houses and people out of your photos and do not park on the road.
