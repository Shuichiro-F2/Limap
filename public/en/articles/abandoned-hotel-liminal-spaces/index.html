Abandoned Hotels as Liminal Spaces: 10 Real Places Frozen in Time, Viewed From Outside

Published: 2026-10-03

Places meant for a single night on the way somewhere, now empty of guests and staff. Abandoned hotels hold the memory of an era when regional tourism thrived. Here are ten you can view from outside the grounds, not for ghost hunting, with where to see them and the rules to keep.

Why Abandoned Hotels Feel Liminal

A hotel is not a place where anyone lives; it is a stopover where you spend one night on the way somewhere. Dozens of identical windows, the same doors lining both sides of a long corridor. When guests and staff are gone, only the stopover remains: a building with no destination.

Large inns in hot-spring towns and hotels built during the ski and resort booms still hold the memory of an era when regional tourism thrived. Because the buildings are big and the places around them are often still in business, they feel as if someone was there a moment ago.

Abandoned hotels, however, tend to be treated as haunted spots, and people keep breaking in to test their nerve. This article covers only places you can see from outside without entering the grounds, or that have official guided tours. It does not describe how to get inside, and you should not go in.

The "Queen of Ruins" on the Mountain: Former Maya Kanko Hotel (Kobe)

The white building on the slopes of Mt. Maya in Kobe began as the Maya Club, built around 1929–30 by the company that ran the Maya Cablecar. Renovated after the war as the Maya Kanko Hotel, it stopped operating as a hotel around 1967 after typhoon damage, served as a student lodge, and fell out of use in 1993.

Its ship-like form jutting out of the mountain greenery earned it the nickname "Queen of Ruins," and in 2021 it became a registered tangible cultural property of Japan. Today you can see the exterior from near Niji Station on the Maya Cablecar, whose sub-name is "Former Maya Kanko Hotel." The interior and its surroundings can be visited only on paid guided tours that fund its preservation.

Big Inns Left in Hot-Spring Towns: Kinugawakan Honten, Sankeien, Sankaikan, Okawa Grand Hotel

At Kinugawa Onsen in Tochigi, large hotels cling to the cliffs along the river. Kinugawakan Honten, an old inn loved for its large bath "Kappa-buro," which began on the riverbank, went bankrupt in 1999 with debts of about 3 billion yen, the first hotel in Kinugawa Onsen to close. Its entrances were sealed in 2022 to keep people out, and in 2026 Nikko City and others set up a council to work toward demolishing the town's abandoned hotels. You can see it from the opposite bank of the Kinugawa River.

In Jozankei Onsen, the hot-spring retreat outside Sapporo, the Hotel Sankeien building stands in the middle of the town. Founded in 1965, it closed in 2014, reopened under another company, and has been shut since February 2020. The Jozankei Hotel right next door has also been closed since 2021, so two large buildings with dark windows stand side by side in the middle of a busy spa town. You can see them from the town's streets, but they are private buildings; do not enter the grounds.

Sankaikan, at Yunoko Onsen in Minamata, Kumamoto, was an inn known for its 30-meter cave bath, billed as Japan's best. With 71 rooms it was one of the largest in the town, but it ceased business in April 2015. It remains on the seafront in its unusual form: an old wooden wing said to be five stories tall, flanked by high-rise wings. You can see it from the neighboring Yunoko Onsen Park. The entrance has no-entry signs and security cameras.

The Okawa Grand Hotel in Higashi-Izu, Shizuoka, is an abandoned hotel on the seafront along Route 135. It is said to have opened in the 1960s and appears to have closed in the mid-2000s. The main building, its windows gone, is clearly visible from the highway, and it was used as a location in the 2024 drama Tokyo Swindlers. Some surrounding structures are reported to have been demolished. The road is busy, so watch for traffic and view it from a safe spot.

Remains of the Ski and Resort Booms: Former Ishiuchi Royal Hotel, Diamond Madarao

Ishiuchi in Minamiuonuma, Niigata, is a ski town within easy reach of Tokyo. Driving along Route 17, you see a castle-like five-story building standing alone on a hill. A hotel for skiers, with arched windows, an external spiral staircase and a pointed tower, it is said to have opened in the 1980s and closed in the mid-2000s. Part of it reportedly burned in a later fire, and the back looks as if demolition stopped halfway. View it only from the highway or public roads.

On the Madarao Plateau in Iiyama, Nagano, stands Diamond Madarao, a white-walled resort hotel. Opened in the mid-1980s, it had some 70 rooms with tennis courts and an outdoor bath and was busy with skiers and student training camps. It is said to have operated until around 2012, and the weight of snow has since damaged its railings and roof. It is visible from Prefectural Route 97 across the plateau, but it is private land, so do not use the access road. Large-scale redevelopment is under way on the plateau, and the view may change.

Two Abandoned Hotels Above the Seto Inland Sea: Hotel La Rainbow, Former Oji Arcadia Resort Hotel

On Mt. Washu in Kurashiki, Okayama, stands Hotel La Rainbow, built when the opening of the Seto Ohashi Bridge (1988) brought a wave of tourists. In 1990 a rotating observation tower, the Rainbow Tower, rising to 138 meters, was added, but the facility closed in 1997. The rusting tower and the building with broken windows can be seen from nearby public roads. The grounds are closed off with barbed wire and signs, and 35 people were caught trespassing and similar offenses in the roughly two years to March 2022.

The former Oji Arcadia Resort Hotel on Mt. Ojigatake in Tamano, Okayama, did not close: it never opened. Built as the first project of a national program to develop facilities in national and quasi-national parks, with about 4 billion yen in government loans under the Resort Law, its seven-story building with two basement levels was completed in 1993. Then the bubble burst, money for the interior never came, and it was abandoned without ever receiving a guest. You can see it from the rooftop observation deck of the Ojigatake rest house. The grounds are off-limits and barricaded.

Bonus: The Gankutsu Hotel, Called a "Hotel" but Never Slept In (Saitama)

In a rocky hill in Yoshimi, Saitama, is a Western-style cave dug by a farmer, Takahashi Minekichi, with chisels and other hand tools for 21 years, from 1904 until his death in 1925. Its formal name is Gankutsu Hotel Kosokan, but it never operated as lodging. The name "hotel" is said to come from onlookers' remark "gankutsu hotteru," a pun meaning "he's digging a cave."

His son carried on the work, and it became a sightseeing spot, but it collapsed in typhoons in 1982 and 1987 and was closed. It is now fenced off and cannot be entered. Part of the exterior is visible from a shop run by his descendants across the road, where old photos are on display. The national historic site of the Yoshimi Hyakuana caves is nearby.

Rules to Keep When Visiting Abandoned Hotels

Every abandoned hotel has an owner. Entering the grounds can mean charges such as trespassing, and people have actually been arrested. A broken fence or an open door does not mean you may go in.

Floors, stairs and walls in old buildings can collapse at any time. People live nearby, and some neighboring inns are still in business. Do not visit at night, make noise or park on the road. View these places from roads, observation decks, opposite banks and other places open to everyone.

If You Find One, Put It on the Map

Once an abandoned hotel is demolished, it is gone for good. Some places in this article already face demolition talks or redevelopment. The regional articles and the article on photographing ruins introduce other places too.

FAQ

Can I go inside an abandoned hotel?

No. Abandoned hotels have owners, and entering without permission can mean charges such as trespassing. At Hotel La Rainbow in Okayama, 35 people were caught in about two years. Buildings can also collapse, so view them from roads, observation decks and other places open to everyone.

Are there abandoned hotels I can tour inside?

At the former Maya Kanko Hotel in Kobe, paid guided tours that fund its preservation are held, and only participants may approach or enter the building. Check the organizers for dates.

Are abandoned hotels haunted spots?

Some are talked about that way, but LIMap introduces them as places to take in the stillness of buildings emptied of people and the traces of an era. Please do not go in to test the rumors.
