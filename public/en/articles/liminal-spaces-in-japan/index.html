Liminal Spaces in Japan: What Makes Them Different

Published: 2026-08-15

"Liminal space" started as an internet term from overseas, but Japan — with its dense rail network and cities that run almost 24 hours a day — happens to be full of ideal liminal spaces. This article covers what makes Japanese liminal spaces distinctive, with concrete examples.

Why Japan Has So Many Liminal Spaces

Japan has one of the densest rail networks in the world, and its cities are packed with stations and underground passageways. In the narrow window between the last train and the first train, stations that are normally packed fall into total silence. This predictable, recurring emptiness — a normally crowded place going quiet at a fixed time — is one of the main reasons Japan has so many liminal spaces.

It also helps that a huge amount of infrastructure built during the Showa and Heisei eras — underground malls, large commercial complexes, housing blocks — is still standing. Uniform tiled floors, fluorescent lighting, green exit signs: the design language of that era, largely unchanged, gives these spaces a distinct feeling of time having stopped.

Category 1: Empty Stations and Underground Passages

Beyond rural stations that are unstaffed all day, even busy city stations empty out completely between the last and first trains. Platforms and connecting corridors stay fully lit and signposted, just with no one walking through them — a textbook example of a liminal space recognized worldwide.

Category 2: Commercial Spaces at Night or Early Morning

Shopping malls after closing, underground shopping streets, supermarkets before opening, food courts in the early morning — these are all classic liminal spaces. Because we know how lively these places normally are, the sight of shuttered storefronts or an empty sales floor lit only by fluorescent lights feels especially jarring.

Category 3: Ruins and Abandoned Facilities

Closed schools, abandoned hospitals, and shuttered amusement parks are deeply tied to Japan's liminal space culture. Japan already had its own urban exploration culture, known as haikyo tanbou (ruins exploration), long before the liminal space trend existed — recording spaces where time seems to have stopped is nothing new here.

Category 4: Danchi and New Towns

The uniform stairwells and corridors of danchi apartment blocks, or a playground with equipment but no children, are also distinctly Japanese liminal spaces. The housing complexes built en masse during Japan's high-growth era have a repetitive quality — rows of identical buildings — that creates an otherworldly atmosphere all on its own.

FAQ

What time of day is best for finding liminal spaces in Japan?

Late night to early morning between the last and first trains, and right after closing or before opening at shops, tend to work best — the bigger the gap from the usual crowd, the more liminal it feels.
