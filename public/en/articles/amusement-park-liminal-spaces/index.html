Liminal Amusement Parks: 9 Real Places, From Department-Store Rooftops to Closed Parks

Published: 2026-10-03

Amusement parks are built on the assumption that crowds will enjoy them. That is why an amusement park without people carries a nostalgia and unease you rarely find elsewhere. From department-store rooftop parks that are still running to parks left standing after closing, here are nine places on LIMap.

Why Empty Amusement Parks Feel So Strange

Rides and music are designed to go with the sound of a crowd. When a ride turns slowly with no one on it, or only faded signs remain, the absence of the people who should be there becomes more noticeable, not less. That is why amusement parks and pools appear so often in liminal-space and dreamcore images.

This article covers both places that are still open and places that have closed and are off-limits. You can enjoy the open ones as usual, but closed parks are private property, so please only look from outside.

Amusement Parks on Department-Store Rooftops

Department stores across Japan once had small amusement parks on their roofs. Losing customers to large suburban theme parks, and with department stores themselves closing, only a few remain. In 2024 the rooftop park of the Hamaya department store in Nagasaki closed, leaving Kyushu without one. Meanwhile, the rooftop park at Matsuzakaya Nagoya reopened in 2025 after a renovation.

On the roof of Tokyu Plaza Kamata in Tokyo is Kamataen, a rooftop amusement park dating back to 1968. Its "Ferris Wheel of Happiness", the only rooftop Ferris wheel in Tokyo, is the third generation: it was due to be removed in 2014, but after calls to keep it, it was reinstalled following renovation. A ride costs 300 yen.

On the roof of Matsuzakaya Takatsuki in Osaka is Skyland, opened with the store in 1979 and said to be the last department-store rooftop amusement park in the Kansai region. Most rides, such as go-karts and a small train on rails, cost 100 yen, and the old rides are kept running with repairs as parts become hard to find.

A Ferris Wheel on a Building, and a Game Corner by a Lake

On the roof of Iyotetsu Takashimaya in Matsuyama is Kururin, a Ferris wheel 85 meters above the ground and 45 meters across. It began running in 2001, when the store was expanded, and is built into the building itself. One revolution takes about 15 minutes, turning slowly above the shoppers below.

By Lake Sagami in Kanagawa is a game corner run by a long-established boat company that dates back to around the time the Sagami Dam was built. Vintage machines such as a 1970s whack-a-mole can be played from 10 yen, and with only electronic sounds echoing, it has a nostalgia unlike an amusement park or a candy store.

Indoor Resorts When No One Is There

At Spa Resort Hawaiians in Fukushima, Spring Town, the swimsuit indoor spa in a Southern-European style within the Spring Park area opened in 1990, drew attention after the official X account shared photos taken when no one was there, noting that people call it "dreamcore" and "liminal space." During opening hours there are other guests, so follow the facility rules and keep other people out of your photos.

Germany's Tropical Islands occupies a hangar built in 1999–2000 to construct airships: 360 meters long, 210 meters wide and 107 meters high, turned into an indoor resort in 2004. Inside it stays around 26°C all year, with a tropical beach and pools under the giant roof. Outside are the plains of northern Germany; inside, the tropics. That gap slowly unsettles your sense of reality.

The Remains of Closed Parks

By Kejonuma pond in Osaki, Miyagi, are the remains of an amusement park that opened in 1979 as Kejonuma Hoyo Land and closed in 2001. A Ferris wheel still stands among the grass, and the site is sometimes used for film and TV shoots. It is private and off-limits, so view it from the municipal road along the pond.

In front of JR Kurahashi Station in Asahi, Chiba, are the remains of Agri Museum Sobu Hana Yuen, a small flower-themed amusement park. It opened only from spring to autumn and was laid out so visitors could tour the flowers by boat along a channel, but it is thought to have closed in the mid-2000s. The gate is shut and there are homes right next door, so please only look quietly from the road in front of the station.

A Park in Vietnam That Came Back From Ruin

Hồ Thủy Tiên, by a lake on the outskirts of Huế, Vietnam, is a water park that opened unfinished in 2004. It drew few visitors, closed and reopened, and was then abandoned for years; the image of a giant dragon-shaped building in the middle of the lake, overgrown with plants, became known worldwide as an "abandoned amusement park."

Huế city began renovating it in 2024, and since 2025 it has been open as a free park with a walking path around the lake. The dragon building still stands, and you can climb stairs up to its mouth. It is a case of a place famous as a ruin becoming a place where people gather again.

Practical Notes for Visiting

Open rooftop parks and Ferris wheels may stop in bad weather. Kamataen is open from 10:00 to 18:00 (until 17:00 in winter), and Skyland at Matsuzakaya Takatsuki is closed when it rains. These are family places, so take care to keep children out of your photos.

The remains of closed parks are all private and off-limits. Entering is trespassing, and buildings may collapse. Please view them only from the spots described in this article.

FAQ

Where can I still find rooftop amusement parks?

This article covers Kamataen in Tokyo (Tokyu Plaza Kamata) and Skyland in Osaka (Matsuzakaya Takatsuki). The rooftop park at Matsuzakaya Nagoya is also open. Hours depend on the weather, so check before you go.

Can I go inside the closed parks?

No. Both the former Kejonuma Leisure Land and Sobu Hana Yuen are private and off-limits. Please only look from outside.

Can I take empty photos at Spa Resort Hawaiians?

The empty photos that went viral were taken by the facility when no one was there. During opening hours there are other guests, so follow the rules and keep other people out of your photos.
