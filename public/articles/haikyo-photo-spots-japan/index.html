「廃墟=心霊スポット」ではない

廃墟と聞くと、真っ先に「肝試し」や「心霊スポット」を連想する人は少なくありません。ですが、実際に廃墟を訪れて写真を撮っている人の多くは、幽霊や怪奇現象には興味がありません。目当てはあくまで、時間が止まったような建物や設備が生み出す独特の質感と、そこにしかない光と影です。

撮影目的で廃墟を訪れるときに気をつけたいこと

Finding Abandoned Buildings on a Map: Japan's Ruins, Without the Haunted-House Angle

Published: 2026-08-26

You want to photograph abandoned buildings, but a haunted-spot pilgrimage isn't really the point. If that sounds like you, this article rounds up photogenic ruins across Japan — for the atmosphere and the architecture, not the ghost stories — with real examples mapped on LIMap.

An Abandoned Building Isn't the Same Thing as a Haunted Spot

Mention an abandoned building, and a lot of people immediately think "haunted spot" or a dare to go inside. But most people who actually visit ruins to take photos aren't interested in ghosts or the supernatural at all. What they're after is the particular texture that comes from a building or piece of equipment being left to time, and the light and shadow found nowhere else.

Interest in these places as industrial heritage has grown too, and they turn up regularly in photo books and exhibitions. This article rounds up ruins worth visiting purely for the photos, with real examples recorded on LIMap.

What to Keep in Mind When Visiting Ruins for Photos

Most abandoned buildings are private property, and entering without permission can count as trespassing. Some are also structurally unsound, with real risk of a floor giving way or part of the structure collapsing. Rather than going in just because it looks possible, it's safer to choose places with an organized viewing tour, or ones you can photograph from a designated lookout or from outside the property line.

The spots introduced on LIMap are simply a record of "places like this exist" — not a recommendation to enter. If you're considering a visit, always check the on-site signage and rules first.

Real Examples Recorded on LIMap

Take the Mikobata Ore Processing Plant Ruins in Hyogo, nicknamed "the Machu Picchu of the East" for the terraced concrete foundations stacked up a mountainside. Or Shima Marineland in Mie, a closed aquarium whose white building still stands quietly overlooking Ago Bay. Or the former coal-washing plant on Ikeshima in Nagasaki, once Japan's last operating undersea coal mine — its rusted sheet-metal roofs and piping stack up the slope in a shape that, from the air, looks almost like one giant machine.
